§1Introduction

This artifact comes from an interactive exercise in the AIML-500 Machine Learning Fundamentals course (assignment 3.3). I worked through four stages with an AI tutor, "Professor Predict," to learn how large language models (LLMs) are trained and what that means for what they can and cannot do reliably. The artifact is my concluding summary from the exercise and my reflection on what I learned. Earlier artifacts show research, building and technical comparison; this one shows that I can critically judge where an AI system is trustworthy. It is written for hiring managers and team leads in data and machine learning.

§3Description

The exercise has four learning stages followed by a personalized summary. The summary and reflection in the document come from my own session.

  1. Be the Model. Without searching or checking anything, I completed sentences the way a language model would, including a trap question about "the current president of Velmora," a country that does not exist. I answered "George."
  2. Reveal the Mechanism. The tutor explained that an LLM's pretraining is built around predicting the next token, and that later post-training helps it follow instructions. Neither step creates a guaranteed fact-checker, so a fluent answer can still be false.
  3. Guess the Training Mix. I sorted kinds of text by how much of each a model is likely to have seen.
  4. Good Fit / Danger Zone. I classified tasks as good fits for an LLM or as needing a source, tool or checking. I placed exact multi-digit multiplication in the Danger Zone.

§4Objective

To show that I understand why language models produce plausible answers rather than verified truth, and that I can decide when an AI tool needs extra support. The main lesson: what a model does well depends on its training material, its later training, and the tools or documents it is given. Fluent text is not proof of accuracy.

§5Process

  1. I read an introductory guide to AI and machine learning from the course.
  2. I pasted the course-provided Professor Predict prompt into an AI chatbot.
  3. I completed all four learning stages and asked questions when something was unclear.
  4. I copied the concluding summary the chatbot generated.
  5. I wrote a reflection on what I learned and added an AI disclosure statement.
  6. For this portfolio, I added an introduction, a stage-by-stage description, an objective and a value proposition, and published the document as a PDF.

§7Value Proposition

Unique Value

I know how to check an AI's answer. This exercise gave me a clear rule: give the model a source document when I want a summary, and check exact numbers with a calculator or code. That habit keeps AI output useful and safe to rely on.

Relevance

Hiring managers in data and machine learning need people who understand where models fail, such as made-up facts, live data and exact arithmetic, and who can explain those limits to colleagues. This artifact shows that judgment, and it connects to my work as a data engineer, where verifying output matters.

§8AI Use Statement

I interacted with an AI chatbot using the course-provided Professor Predict prompt to complete the four learning stages and generate the concluding summary. An AI tool was also used to review grammar and check word count for my personal reflection, which reflects my own responses and takeaways from the exercise.
